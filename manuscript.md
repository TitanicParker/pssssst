# The Three Pages and the Nine-Year Record

## How a doctor's handwritten interview became a lasting explanation for painful feet

*A full-length, source-anchored manuscript for a new reader. The narrative below uses ordinary language; the detailed documentary sections preserve the source wording, clinical reference numbers and important uncertainties. The handwritten image accompanying this manuscript was written by the neurologist in the course of interviewing the patient, not by the patient.*

**How to check sources.** The clinical identifiers are collected in the source appendix at the end. The handwritten interview image is separately preserved because its visual source controls the precise handwriting. A reference identifier is a retrieval address, not a guarantee that the underlying record independently proves every inference made about it. Reports of what the patient remembers telling the neurologist are labelled as recollections. The absence of an identified entry is not asserted to establish that no assessment ever happened.

**A straightforward statement of the issue.** A doctor wrote down that his patient had begun to notice a difference between foot pain and leg tension. A later typed account said he had realised they were different problems. The final report described him as having 'markedly divorced' them in his own mind, while proposing that the painful feet might be a separate condition. The subsequent record, however, repeatedly brought the feet back into contact with abnormal movement and neurological medication. The long inquiry is about how that stronger meaning arose, what medical reasoning supported it, and why later evidence did or did not change it.

# Part I — The story in plain language
# What happened to my feet in the 2017 neurological record

## How an account of different feelings became a statement about different medical problems

*An account for a reader encountering this case for the first time. The story is told plainly. Exact clinical wording, its source locations and the limitations of the evidence are kept alongside it.*

### The two accounts

*Source: original handwritten neurologist interview notes.*
This story begins with two records of a conversation at a hospital in July 2017.

The first record was handwritten by the neurologist during an interview with the patient. The second was a typed hospital account of the same history. There was later a final neurological impression.

When the three are placed beside each other, something important changes.

In the handwritten record, the neurologist noted that the patient had begun to notice that pain in his feet and tension in his legs did not feel the same. The typed history said the patient had *realised* they were not the same *problem*. The final account went further still. It said the patient had **“markedly divorced”** the two in his own mind.

This matters because noticing two different sensations is not the same as knowing they have separate medical causes. A person can tell the difference between a painful corn and muscle tension without knowing whether the way the muscles contract or the way they walk contributes to the corn.

The same hospital report also suggested that the painful feet might be *completely separate* from the Parkinsonian movement disorder the hospital had discovered. It raised the possibility of peripheral neuropathy, perhaps connected with past alcohol use. Yet it also said the explanation was **“Not clear.”**

So the question is not only why the wording changed. It is whether that stronger account of the patient's own understanding helped the hospital separate the painful feet from a neurological problem whose relationship to the feet had not yet been explained.

No one needs to assume deliberate deception to ask that question. We can see the changes in the records. What we still need is the clinical evidence that justified them.

### What the handwritten record actually says

*Source: handwritten clinician interview notes (native image).*
The neurologist was taking a history of many years of physical difficulty. His handwritten notes included a description of **“physical tension in my arms & legs”**, referring back to 2014. They recorded that the present condition was the **“maturation of that”** and that symptoms had been noticed in the **“Legs/Body.”**

Only after that did the account turn to corns, calluses and fallen arches. At one point the note reads **“As I look @ it now the next issue was”**, followed by the foot problems.

The key passage concerns the period after the patient left bar work in 2012. The handwritten entry says:

> “for the most part I took pain in my feet out of picture”

And then:

> “Began to be aware that foot pain & leg tension weren’t the same…”

These words describe the patient's experience. His foot pain had become less prominent after leaving work that kept him on his feet, and he became more conscious of the tension in his legs.

**They do not themselves diagnose two independent illnesses.** They do not say that neurological movement played no part in the feet. They do not say the patient knew that his foot pain came from damaged peripheral nerves.

![Original handwritten interview page](2017_handwritten_interview_evidence.png)

One point must be kept exact: **this was the neurologist's handwriting, not the patient's handwriting**. It is the earliest surviving written version of the relevant interview, but it may not reproduce every spoken word. If later wording came from further discussion or findings, those sources must be identified rather than assumed absent.

### What changed when it was typed


The typed hospital account says that leaving bar work:

> “seemed to improve the pain in the feet which reduced to a degree ‘out of the picture’”

It then says:

> “Therefore in 2012 he began to realise that the foot pain and the leg tension were not the same problem because: as the leg pain settled — he was more aware of the tension.”

Look closely at the steps. **“Began to be aware”** became **“began to realise.”** **“Weren’t the same”** became **“were not the same problem.”** The words **“Therefore”** and **“because”** made the account sound more like a settled explanation. And at the end, the typed wording referred to **“leg pain”** even though the distinction being discussed was between *foot pain* and *leg tension*.

These are not just spelling or punctuation changes. They affect how a later reader understands what the patient knew. The first note records a difference in how symptoms were experienced. The second reads more like a conclusion about how the problems should be classified.

It remains possible that the typed version reflected something further said in the interview. That possibility should be checked. What is certain is that the two surviving written versions do not say exactly the same thing.

### What the final report said


The final neurological impression put the foot symptoms into a new possible explanation. It stated:

> “The leg symptoms including corns and callouses and burning may have been completely separate or perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy perhaps in somewhat respect related to the significant original alcohol intake? Not clear as this has improved.”

Then it said the patient had:

> “in his own mind - markedly divorced the feelings of tension in the legs which was/is the predominant problem - from the ‘corns and callouses’ issues and Pains in the feet.”

The first sentence is plainly uncertain: **“may,” “perhaps,” “Not clear.”** It offers a possibility rather than establishing peripheral neuropathy as the cause. The second sentence is much stronger about what the patient supposedly thought. It attributes a pronounced separation to him.

The exact question is therefore simple: **where did that extra certainty about the patient's own view come from?** What additional conversation, examination or clinical reasoning turned the earlier wording into **“markedly divorced”**?

Peripheral neuropathy is a real neurological condition and could coexist with Parkinsonism. Nothing in this argument excludes it. The issue is whether the available record demonstrates why it was used to help explain a particular set of painful foot problems, and why those symptoms could be treated as sufficiently separate from the abnormal gait, muscle tone and foot posture already observed.

### Why the feet were in a neurological investigation in the first place


The patient did not arrive at the hospital with nothing but an isolated corn.

On **10 April 2017**, his GP recorded pain in both feet and an abnormal gait, with mallet toes, calluses and pressure areas. The patient himself had considered whether an earlier sciatica problem had influenced his walking. The GP referred him to Podiatry, but did not stop there.

By **8 June**, the GP was considering a neurological opinion on his **“gait, feet and ‘muscle tension’ issues.”** On **12 June**, an occupational-health doctor also raised concern about the gait. By **29 June**, the GP had recorded unusual stiffness, increased tone, difficulty starting movements, loss of dexterity, toe-clawing and an urgent concern about **“?parkinsonian or other underlying diagnosis.”**

The significance is ordinary. The GP had seen the feet and the way the patient moved as part of the same unresolved clinical situation. The referral asked Neurology to investigate, not to accept in advance that the painful feet were a separate disease.

This did not prove one cause for every symptom. It meant the relationship needed to be worked out.

### What Neurology found


During the July 2017 admission, Neurology recognised severe Parkinsonism at an unusually young age. The notes described **“Gross bradykinesia”**—marked slowness of movement—and a **“strikingly abnormal”** gait.

A left-leg movement looked like foot drop, a term commonly used when a person cannot lift the front of the foot normally. But the neurological note said the appearance was **“somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”** Elsewhere it recorded **“LLL Kicks out like a foot drop.”**

Neurology also recorded flattened arches and hammertoes. The GP had already mentioned toe-clawing.

These facts do not prove that abnormal movement caused the calluses. But they make clear why the relationship between movement and painful foot loading mattered. The feet were part of a body whose movement was demonstrably abnormal.

Neurology also treated the leg tension as part of its care. The discharge account says:

> “Clonazepam was given at night to relieve tension in the legs.”

The plan then considered whether procyclidine, also called Kemadrin, might be tolerated well enough to replace the clonazepam at night.

That treatment plan does not prove which mechanism caused the pain or tension. It shows that Neurology had accepted a significant lower-limb symptom as something to treat neurologically, before the final report raised the possibility that the painful feet belonged to a separate branch.

### The question raised before discharge

*Clinical anchors: (patient account; not contemporaneous corroboration).*
The patient reports that, before leaving hospital, he asked to see the neurologist because he believed his feet had not been adequately investigated. He used an analogy about a car passing an NCT inspection but being sent out on bald tyres: the major assessment had been done, but an important part of the original problem had been left unresolved.

According to his recollection, the neurologist's substantive answer was **“Podiatry.”**

This is **patient-reported evidence**, not a confirmed quotation from a contemporaneous clinical entry. Any witness, student-doctor account or original note of the exchange should be sought. The distinction matters.

The patient's point, as he describes it, was not that he knew all foot pain came from Parkinsonism. Nor was he objecting to a final written theory he had not yet read. He was saying that the investigation of the feet felt unfinished.

Podiatry could properly treat corns, calluses, abnormal pressure and structural problems. But sending a person to Podiatry did not, on its own, answer whether abnormal neurological walking or toe posture contributed to those problems. The question was who would make that connection, or explain why there was none.

The surviving final synthesis does not record the patient's reported challenge in those terms. Whether other records of that conversation survive remains open.

### How alcohol acquired another role in the story

The hospital history recorded that the patient had used alcohol to ease physical tension. One note called it **“DE-TENSIONING THERAPY.”**

Later, the final impression also raised alcohol as a possible contributor to peripheral neuropathy affecting the feet.

These two possibilities can coexist. Someone might drink to ease a symptom and also have health problems associated with alcohol. There is no automatic contradiction.

But the two statements do different jobs. One describes the patient's reason for drinking, as understood by the clinician. The other suggests a possible medical cause of foot symptoms.

The question is what evidence supported that second explanation in this particular patient. Similarly, **“burning”** appears in the final impression, but the source of that descriptor needs to be traced. The absence of the word from the handwritten passage being compared does not prove the patient never said it elsewhere.

These are questions about where the clinical explanation came from, not accusations that words were deliberately invented.

### What happened when neurological medication was withdrawn

After discharge, the patient received neurological treatment that brought substantial relief to tension and movement.

During a later period off procyclidine, he reported that tension grew, his feet became restless and his walking deteriorated. In April 2018, the neurologist recorded that the first thing the patient noticed changing rapidly was:

> “the return on the foot, of the outside left corn”

The neurologist also recorded that he became more **“shifty”** and **“unrelaxed”** without procyclidine.

It would be wrong to claim from that sentence alone that a medication removed and then regenerated a corn. The reported change could have involved pain, irritation, pressure, prominence or another feature of the same foot territory. The record does not identify its exact mechanism.

But it is still an important observation. A foot phenomenon was reported to change in the same withdrawal period as the patient's movement and tension symptoms. That was a reason to ask whether the original separation needed fresh investigation.

This was **one withdrawal episode**, described in records from 2017 and 2018—not two independent experiments.

### What Podiatry found in the foot

Podiatry recorded genuine, recurring painful pressure lesions, including an area near the **left fifth metatarsophalangeal joint**—the joint at the base of the little toe. Treatment included removing hardened skin and trying to reduce pressure through offloading and insoles.

Those findings matter because the foot problem was real. This case does not require denying mechanical disease. Podiatry was treating actual painful tissue.

But treating the site of a pressure lesion is not necessarily the same as explaining why pressure keeps returning there. Neurology had information about the patient's abnormal movement and medication response. Podiatry had information about the particular sore location.

**What still needs to be demonstrated is the joined-up assessment**, if one occurred, explaining whether those findings were related. The general outside-left-foot description in the neurological note and the specific Podiatry site should be compared carefully; their exact identity must not simply be assumed.

### The GP brought the connection back in 2019

A 2019 Podiatry referral said previous foot problems related to **“increased tone and abnormal gait relating to Parkinson's disease.”** It also described **“clawing feet”** and pain in a person who developed Parkinsonism at a very young age.

This was not proof that Parkinsonism caused each lesion. It was another clinician explicitly connecting the feet with the movement disorder.

A risk-screen on the form marked **“No neuropathy.”** That tick-box is not a specialist exclusion of neuropathy, and other screening fields also differ from structural findings in older notes. Its limited status must be respected.

The 2019 referral matters because the integrated explanation was still present in the patient's care. Whether the neurologist personally saw that document is an additional question and should not be presumed merely because it existed.

### In 2020 Neurology made a foot-movement distinction explicitly

In 2020, Neurology again examined a footdrop-like movement. The clinician recorded normal strength in the relevant directions of foot and ankle movement and concluded:

> “Coming back to the foot throw which looks a little bit like a foot drop—this is actually parkinsonian because the power for dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion are all normal.”

The note shows the reasoning for calling that *movement* Parkinsonian. It does not show that every foot pain was caused by Parkinsonism. Normal strength does not by itself settle every neurological question.

But it is a useful comparison. Neurology was capable of examining a confusing-looking lower-limb sign, checking the relevant function and recording why the sign was classified one way rather than another.

The unresolved issue was how that recognised neurological motor problem related, if at all, to pain and recurring structural lesions.

### The question was still alive in 2024 and 2025

Later records described different responses among pain, toe curling and movement symptoms. In 2024, pregabalin helped pain without resolving toe curling. That suggests different symptom components, but not necessarily independent diseases.

In 2025, the neurologist recorded that the feet were the patient's dominant concern. Mornings were **“murder in the feet”**, and the first footsteps were extremely painful. The note also stated:

> “Once the Parkinsons medications hit his system he does have a relief in the feelings in the feet.”

Yet peripheral neuropathy was still mentioned conditionally:

> “If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here…”

The clinician did not simply say that pain relief proved direct Parkinsonian pain. That caution is medically important. Medication might change pain through movement, posture, loading or other pathways.

But the record had now accumulated years of observations that called for an explanation of how the parts fitted together. What integrated reconsideration was documented? If the separation remained appropriate, what findings supported keeping it?

### Why repeated letters are not automatically new proof

Some later letters were written by the same clinician involved in the original hospital history. That continuity matters. It may give the clinician more knowledge of the case, not less.

But when an earlier interpretation is repeated, repetition does not by itself prove it was properly established at the beginning. A statement can survive because it is familiar, even when later evidence requires that it be checked again.

This is not proof that the clinician ignored contrary information. It is a reason to examine the letters carefully: **what new information was recorded, what was actually considered, and what decision followed?**

For documents sent between different services, the analysis must also distinguish a letter existing from a particular clinician receiving or reading it.

### What the case does and does not establish

**What the records show:** the foot and movement problems were initially considered together; the handwritten interview note, typed history and final impression use progressively stronger words about the patient's supposed separation of them; the discharge impression considered possible neuropathy and an alcohol connection while expressly recording uncertainty; Neurology treated leg tension; and later clinicians recorded repeated observations involving foot symptoms and neurological movement or treatment.

**What is not yet proved:** that the doctor deliberately misrepresented the patient; that peripheral neuropathy was absent; that every corn was caused by dystonia or Parkinsonism; that a drug directly changed the structure of a corn; that no assessment took place anywhere beyond the documents reviewed; or that any particular professional failing caused a specific injury.

There are two separate questions to resolve.

**First:** What evidence justified the stronger description of the patient's understanding and the possible separation of the painful feet in 2017?

**Second:** When the later record repeatedly showed foot symptoms changing with movement or neurological treatment, what examination and reasoning were used to test the original explanation?

The hospital may be able to identify further contemporaneous evidence. If so, it should be examined. If it cannot, that matters too. But a gap in the reviewed documents should be described accurately, not inflated into absolute knowledge about everything that did or did not happen.

### The question at the heart of it

The patient told the neurologist about different feelings. The neurologist's handwritten interview note recorded that limited distinction. The typed account made it sound like a conclusion about different problems. The final impression said the patient had **“markedly divorced”** the two.

At the same time, the hospital recognised serious neurological abnormalities affecting the patient's legs and walking, and suggested a possible separate explanation for the painful feet.

**What medical evidence justified that separation, and what happened when later evidence repeatedly called it into question?**

That is what an independent review should answer.

### 5A. Maturation, treatment and the clinical history before it was reconstructed

**Primary-source anchors:** handwritten interview, 3 July 2017 (native image; typed counterpart); GP referral; examination; clonazepam/procyclidine plan; discharge account; DaTscan request; Professor Healy's 2017 opinion; withdrawal correspondence. The separate genetics discussion in the discharge document should be compared with the native letter and its dating and sign-off history.

**The continuity present at the first encounter.** In the neurologist's handwritten interview, the patient describes having attended the unit in 2014 with **“physical tension in my arms & legs”** and describes the current condition as the **“maturation of that.”** The wider history places the beginnings of physical symptoms around 2004. Here *maturation* does a specific job: it connects an earlier bodily experience to the current neurological presentation in the patient's own account, as written down by the neurologist. It is not a retrospective PRKN diagnosis. It is a contemporaneous assertion of continuity that belongs in the source history.

The GP's observations provide another view of the same evolving clinical terrain. In April 2017, bilateral foot problems and abnormal gait were already present together. By late June, the referral described increased tone in upper and lower limbs, difficulty relaxing, toe clawing, movement-initiation problems and functional deterioration. The words *feet* and *tension* did not have to mean the same thing for their relationship to require investigation: the GP had referred them as components of an unresolved presentation. At hospital, Neurology recorded hammertoes, pes planus and a left-lower-limb gait appearance **“somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture — NOT FOOT DROP.”**

**An intervening clinical action.** During that admission, the neurologist commenced clonazepam at night for tension. The discharge account later specifies **“tension in the legs”**; the treatment plan contemplates whether nocturnal procyclidine might allow clonazepam to be stopped. The prescription did not wait for the later, sharpened account of how foot pain and leg tension should be classified. It documents actual symptomatic treatment within the same encounter that had preserved the patient's longstanding history of arm-and-leg tension. A medicine prescribed for tension does not by itself establish dystonia or the cause of painful corns, but the action fixes a useful contemporary comparator: **what the clinician heard and treated before the final explanatory partition acquired its written form.**

**What changed in representation.** The typed history does not retain the explicit *“maturation of that”* statement in the form found in the handwritten note. Meanwhile **“Began to be aware that foot pain & leg tension weren’t the same”** becomes **“began to realise”** they were **“not the same problem”**, supported by added connective and causal language. The impression then says the patient had **“markedly divorced”** tension from the painful feet and entertains a separate or peripheral-neuropathy explanation. These documentary steps do not demonstrate that further clinical reasoning never occurred. They show where the surviving record makes its change of emphasis most visible: the historical connection is less explicit, while separation becomes more assertive and is attributed to the patient.

The distinction is not between a real patient and a fictitious one. It is between **the patient as encountered, observed and treated** and **the subsequently authored representation used to support the final impression**. Because the same neurologist wrote the initial history, directed treatment and contributed to the later synthesis, the contemporaneous treatment record can be read against the transformed account. The relevant question is what positive findings, beyond a strengthened retelling of the patient's symptom distinction, justified moving painful foot phenomena outside the neurological lower-limb problem then being examined.

**The management-and-knowledge loop.** The discharge genetics discussion expressly asks whether a firm genetic diagnosis would change management and gives anticipated lack of immediate management change as a reason to consider delaying testing. At the same time, Professor Healy suspected PARK2/PRKN, and the neurologist's own discussion allowed for targeted testing. This establishes an explicit decision rule about *genetic confirmation*: its immediate value was considered partly in terms of an already envisaged treatment approach. It does **not** establish that genetic testing and detailed examination of the lower-limb phenotype were interchangeable, nor that delay in one justified neglect of the other. That is precisely the distinction the record must preserve. If the plan was settled while the relationships among dystonic-looking gait, toe posture, loading, pain and tension remained uncharacterised, knowledge capable of revising the plan risked being evaluated from within the plan itself.

**The return of the question through treatment.** In the September/October 2017 procyclidine withdrawal, the patient's contemporaneous report described building tension, restless feet and deterioration in walking. The neurologist's April 2018 account of **the same withdrawal episode** records that the first rapidly changing phenomenon concerned the outside-left-foot corn, and that the patient became more **“shifty”** and **“unrelaxed”** off treatment. The two accounts should not be counted as two experiments. Nor does the rapid change prove that a corn physically disappeared and re-formed. It does show that foot experience and the movement/tension state changed within one medication perturbation, after neurological treatment had already been made part of the case. In the setting of an existing PARK2/PRKN hypothesis, this is an especially concrete opportunity to test the original clinical separation against the patient's continuing course.

**The resulting source-to-management question.** The handwritten *maturation* statement, the GP's account of progression, the neurologist's first examination and prescription, the modified typed history, the discharge partition, the PRKN hypothesis and the later withdrawal observation form one traceable sequence. Its critical issue is not whether every manifestation had one cause. It is **whether a newly authoritative representation of separated problems displaced an earlier continuity that the clinician had both recorded and acted upon, and whether later treatment-state evidence was used to reconsider that representation**. This is the loop to audit: original history → clinical action → rewritten clinical account → management framed around that account → new patient-specific observations → documented reconsideration, or its absence from the available record.

### 5B. The same lower-limb territory is absorbed into treatment, then segregated in explanation

**Primary sources:** discharge summary; discharge medication list; treatment plan; 2018 Rivotril record; 2024 neurology note.

The most revealing feature of the treatment record is how naturally Neurology initially **absorbed the lower-limb tension into neurological care**. The discharge summary does not hedge about why clonazepam was used: **“Clonazepam was given at night to relieve tension in the legs.”** In the same discharge record, clonazepam appears as an active medication at **0.25 mg nocte**. The longer treatment plan then says that, if nocturnal Kemadrin/procyclidine were tolerated, **“it may be possible to remove the clonazepam.”** The most conservative reading is not that clonazepam proved dystonia, but that Neurology had already accepted the leg-tension phenomenon as a legitimate symptomatic target inside its Parkinsonian treatment architecture.

That makes the later documentary segregation unusually important. The record does not show the lower-limb phenomenon being rejected as non-neurological before treatment. It shows the opposite: it is observed, treated, followed and pharmacologically linked to another Parkinsonian therapy. Only afterwards does the typed/final synthesis divide the history, describing the patient as having **“markedly divorced”** leg tension from the corns, callouses and foot pain, while allowing the latter to sit under a possible peripheral-neuropathy explanation. In other words, **therapeutic ownership remains neurological while explanatory ownership narrows.**

**Meaning.** This is the tension that should be made explicit. The leg-tension phenotype was sufficiently neurological to justify clonazepam, sufficiently related to Parkinsonian treatment that procyclidine might replace it, and sufficiently persistent that clonazepam/Rivotril continued to appear in the later record. Yet the foot-side of the same lower-limb history was simultaneously being separated into a peripheral branch. That is not proof that both were one mechanism. It is proof that the record maintained two different conceptual treatments of overlapping lower-limb territory and therefore owed itself an explanation for the boundary.

The continuity matters. In 2018 the neurologist records ongoing Rivotril use, estimating the dose at approximately 0.5 mg and noting that the patient sometimes used a half-tablet and experienced next-morning benefit for neck/back symptoms. ### The effect of continuity on the documentary ratchet

The handwritten-to-typed progression is already important because it shows a source statement becoming more definite: **“began to be aware”** becomes **“began to realise”**; **“weren't the same”** becomes **“were not the same problem”**; then the impression says the phenomena were **“markedly divorced.”** Continuity makes that progression more consequential because the stronger formulation is not merely inherited by an unrelated future reader. It is created and then later encountered within the same clinical relationship. The same people are therefore positioned to know both the weaker source formulation and the stronger downstream one.

This is the point at which the case becomes about more than a single questionable phrase. A **documentary ratchet** can occur when observation is successively re-expressed as interpretation, interpretation as proposition, and proposition as inherited history. Once the stronger wording is embedded in a discharge summary, later notes can treat the partition as part of the patient's established background rather than as a conclusion whose evidential basis still needs to be tested. Continuity means that this ratchet cannot be explained simply by institutional amnesia. The same participants remain available to compare the inherited proposition with the original facts.

**The effect can be stated plainly:** same patient; same GP; same neurologist; changing documentary meaning. That gives the chronology a built-in control. The question is not who misunderstood somebody else's old note. The question is when, why and on what evidence the meaning of the same phenotype changed within a stable clinical relationship.

### What that says about reopening

Continuity also raises the standard for reopening. A later clinician who has never met the patient may reasonably accept a prior consultant's partition unless something obviously contradicts it. The same neurologist who made or adopted the partition is differently situated. When later treatment-state evidence crosses the boundary, when lower-limb phenomena are again classified as Parkinsonian, or when Parkinson medication repeatedly changes the experience of the feet, the clinician is not encountering a wholly new case. He is encountering evidence capable of testing his own earlier model.

That is why the absence of a clearly documented **retrial of the partition itself** matters. The record contains repeated reassessment of symptoms, medications and alternative explanations, but the foundational question—whether the foot territory had ever been validly separated from the broader neurological lower-limb phenotype—does not appear to receive an equally explicit re-examination. The continuity of patient, GP and neurologist makes that omission harder to attribute to simple discontinuity of care.

### The case stated after the evidence

In April 2017, a GP saw bilateral foot problems and an abnormal stiff/festinant gait in the same patient. By 29 June, that GP had observed increased tone, inability to relax, toe clawing and progressive motor dysfunction and deliberately sent the patient for neurological assessment. On 3 July, the patient’s handwritten history placed longstanding leg/body tension before the foot pathology and said only that, after leaving bar work largely removed the foot pain from prominence, he **“Began to be aware that foot pain & leg tension weren’t the same.”** Neurology then saw a strikingly abnormal, dystonic-looking lower-limb gait and hammertoes and treated leg tension neurologically. Alcohol was explicitly described as a **“de-tensioning therapy”**; clonazepam was explicitly given **“to relieve tension in the legs”**; and procyclidine was considered capable of replacing clonazepam. When the history became typed prose, however, the patient’s words were strengthened: “I took pain in my feet out of picture” became improvement only “to a degree”; “began to be aware” became “began to realise”; “weren’t the same” became “not the same problem”; “Therefore” and “because” were supplied; and “leg pain” appeared at the explanatory hinge. The impression then added or foregrounded “burning”, possible peripheral neuropathy, possible alcohol causation and the attribution that the patient had “markedly divorced” the foot pain from the leg tension. Crucially, alcohol changed explanatory role: a historical fact first used to explain how the patient tried to relieve tension was now also used to support a possible cause for the newly separated peripheral branch, while clonazepam/procyclidine remained inside neurological treatment architecture. **The phenotype did not merely accumulate facts; it was reorganised as new interpretation arrived.** That partition thereafter acquired clinical life. Yet the same record subsequently documented neurological medication withdrawal changing a foot phenomenon together with a “shifty/unrelaxed” state, a podiatry referral reconnecting foot pain and clawing with increased tone and abnormal Parkinsonian gait, a pseudo-footdrop explicitly classified as “actually parkinsonian”, OFF-related interpretation of leg pain and shuffling, and later relief of foot symptoms when Parkinson medication reached the system. None of those later observations proves a single mechanism. But the continuity of the case changes their force: the patient remains the same, the GP remains the same, and the neurologist remains the same. This is therefore not primarily a story of handover drift between strangers. It is a single clinical relationship repeatedly encountering evidence capable of testing its own earlier partition. Together, those later observations make one question unavoidable: **what evidence originally earned the separation, what documentary reallocations made it persuasive, and what evidence allowed it to remain operative after the same longitudinal record repeatedly challenged it?**

# Part III — Following the thread rather than assuming the answer

## A note about the three handwritten pages

The three pages matter because they are the earliest surviving clinical record of the relevant interview. Their value is not measured by how many pages they occupy. A single sentence can influence what a later doctor thinks was already established. If that sentence is then paraphrased, interpreted and included in a discharge impression, its new wording can accompany the patient through many years of treatment. That is the particular risk under examination here. The words on the pages must be read literally and compared with the later statements about them. The notes are written by the clinician, not by the patient, and they are not certified as a complete word-for-word transcript of the conversation. 

A careful review must allow that a physician could have received further information verbally that did not appear on those three pages. It must also allow that the typed account may have been prepared through transcription or dictation. Neither possibility erases the difference visible in the surviving documents. It changes the next question: what other source, if any, supports the stronger language? The investigation must seek that source before treating the added words as either a justified clinical interpretation or an unsupported insertion. 

## The difference between two feelings and two causes

Many people distinguish symptoms in daily language. A person may describe a stiff leg, a burning foot, a sore corn and a cramped toe as four separate experiences. They may even say that one complaint was obscured by another. That observation tells a doctor something valuable about timing, intensity and perception. It does not decide whether all four complaints share one disease, whether one mechanical process aggravates another, or whether two or more disorders coexist. This is the exact interpretive boundary crossed by the disputed wording. The original note is about awareness; the typed narrative says different problems; the final impression attributes a forceful separation to the patient. 

A proper explanation might still conclude that the problems were partly separate. For example, local pressure lesions can be real regardless of why the pressure is there, and peripheral nerve disease could coexist with Parkinsonism. The criticism is not that a doctor considered multiple possible mechanisms. It is that the medical account should show why this patient's feet were separated to the degree implied by the final narrative, and what was to happen while that conclusion remained provisional. The word **“Not clear”** is itself a reminder that the question had not been closed by the sentence proposing neuropathy. 

## A patient's warning and a later written account

The patient says he raised his concern before discharge and was directed to Podiatry. His analogy about the car inspection is powerful because its meaning is not technical: it asks how an investigation can be pronounced complete while an obvious part of the original complaint remains outstanding. It is important not to rewrite that event retrospectively. He was not objecting to a final letter that he had already studied; he was protesting the perceived incompleteness of the clinical work. The evidence of that conversation presently comes from his account and requires appropriate corroboration. 

The issue raised by the omission is equally specific. A future reader consulting the discharge synthesis sees the doctor's final theory, but does not see the patient's reported objection to discharge with the foot question still open. This may affect how later clinicians understand the history of disagreement and the intended limits of the assessment. It is not, by itself, proof that the omission was purposeful. It is reason to ask whether there were other notes, witnesses, discussions or referrals that recorded the unresolved question. 

## What the treatment withdrawal can and cannot show

The 2017 withdrawal communication and the April 2018 retrospective note refer to the same period off procyclidine. They should not be described as two independent experiments. During that period the patient reported worsening tension, more restless feet and deteriorating walking. The neurologist later reported that the first rapid change involved a corn phenomenon on the outside of the left foot. This is the kind of combined observation that should put a rigid dividing line under examination. It does not independently show what happened to the physical lesion, or prove that the drug acted directly upon callus tissue. 

The strongest version of this evidence is therefore measured: a change in neurological medication was associated in the clinical history with change in a foot phenomenon and in movement/tension symptoms. A reviewer should ask exactly what was observed, what was self-reported, whether examination of the lesion occurred at that point, and whether a change in loading, posture, sensation or pain could explain the sequence. The chronology is important; the mechanism remains open. 

## Why the recurring left-foot site deserves close attention

A painful callus is not merely a word in a summary. Podiatry found repeated pathology at a named anatomical site, the left fifth metatarsophalangeal joint. Its treatment involved local debridement and offloading. Those details matter because they show genuine structural care, but also expose a separate question about the origin of loading. If the patient's gait and toe position were neurologically abnormal, the relationship between movement and pressure was at least worth considering. The record must be read to find out whether anyone actually considered it. 

The precise identity of the lesion reported during drug withdrawal and the lesions treated in Podiatry must not be assumed from the phrase 'outside left foot' alone. The similarities are grounds for a site-to-site comparison, not an entitlement to call them objectively identical. A useful evidential table would compare dates, site descriptions, laterality, treatment, timing relative to medication changes and whether the treating clinician had access to the other specialty's notes. 

## The words in 2019 that change the reading of 2017

The 2019 referral explicitly described feet problems in relation to increased tone and abnormal gait associated with Parkinson's disease. That matters because it shows a later clinician did not necessarily inherit the idea that the feet were simply a detached peripheral disease. The screening field saying 'No neuropathy' must remain in its proper setting. It is not a nerve-conduction result or a specialist exclusion of all neuropathy. It is nevertheless significant that the form did not describe established neuropathy as the settled reason for the problem. 

Access matters. The later GP document existed in the patient's care, but that is not the same as demonstrating that the neurologist personally read it. A reader should know which medical professional wrote a document, where it was sent and whether it appeared in a subsequent consultation. This distinction separates 'information existed somewhere in the system' from 'the doctor knew and consciously considered this finding.' 

## The question that follows into 2025

By 2025, the foot pain was not a small residual complaint. It was prominent enough for the neurologist to identify it as the central thing on the patient's mind. The patient described severe pain on first walking and relief once Parkinson's medication became effective. The clinician continued to discuss neuropathy conditionally. The direct record therefore contains both an apparent neurological medication relationship and continuing uncertainty about the cause of pain. That combination asks for integration, not an automatic declaration for either side. 

A longitudinal medical record can accumulate many statements without repeatedly revisiting their original evidential basis. Consistency of wording across time may reflect clinical continuity. It is not necessarily independent confirmation, especially where the same author remains important in the record. At the same time, external specialist advice and independent GP or Podiatry notes must be credited for what they actually establish. Each source has a proper evidential reach. 

## What an answer from the institution would look like

A meaningful answer would identify the source of each changed term, explain the positive evidence for the possible peripheral diagnosis, and show the documented thinking about the motor and mechanical alternatives. It would also identify any patient warning, the handover question passed to Podiatry, the later observations that reached Neurology and the occasions on which the original explanation was tested or changed. The answer could support the original decision, partially support it or demonstrate that some step was missing. This manuscript does not decide the expert clinical standard in advance. 

The point of the long story is not to turn three pages into more evidence than they contain. It is to trace the consequences of how those pages were interpreted. The story extends because the clinical problem continued, the same foot territory recurred in specialist care, neurological movement remained abnormal, medication responses accumulated, and later doctors continued to describe the feet in terms that reopened the original relationship. The handwritten record anchors the inquiry; the later years test what was made of it.
