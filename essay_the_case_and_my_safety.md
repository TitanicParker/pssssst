# The case begins with my feet, but culminates in my safety

*An essay in progress — expanded evidence-led and adversarial draft, 9 October 2026*

## 1. The changing nature of suffering and the absence of a visible patient-safety response

For approximately thirteen years, from 2004 to 2017, I lived with foot pain. It was a longstanding physical problem, but it had not previously caused me psychological distress of the kind that later emerged. That distinction is fundamental.

The identification of Parkinsonism in 2017 introduced neurological treatment that began to modify my experience of my feet. Kemadrin (procyclidine) provided relief, and its withdrawal was associated with the return of a left-foot problem alongside worsening neurological tension and movement. Later, Sinemet and other dopaminergic treatment affected my foot symptoms and pain.

The point is not that every foot symptom had one neurological cause, nor that any particular medication has been proved to have caused the later deterioration. It is that **the foot pain was no longer simply the longstanding condition with which I had lived before 2017. Its relationship to neurological medication had become clinically observable, and the nature and consequences of the suffering changed profoundly.**

By 2023, my GP recorded that the pain was **“causing death wish,”** expressed concern for my welfare and requested urgent review before matters became more serious. Movement-disorders nursing recorded that I had **“no quality of life.”** By March 2025, the suffering had reached the point where I was expressing that I was not willing to continue living in that condition.

These were not merely descriptions of pain severity. They were explicit signals that the clinical problem had become a **patient-safety problem**, involving severe physical suffering, psychological endurance and the possibility of self-harm.

The record does document clinical activity: urgent requests for neurological review, medication adjustments, pain treatment and subsequent recognition of the relationship between Parkinson's medication and the foot symptoms. Those actions must be acknowledged.

But **within the record examined, no distinct, documented suicide-risk assessment, crisis or psychiatric referral, safety plan, coordinated safeguarding intervention, or sustained psychological-risk follow-up has been identified in response to those warnings.**

That absence requires investigation in its own right. It cannot be answered simply by demonstrating that neurological appointments occurred, medications were prescribed, or alternative diagnoses were considered.

The events of March 2025 make the question more pressing. The reported profound improvement following Sinemet CR was not only a therapeutic development. It provided further evidence that neurological treatment could materially change the experience that had become psychologically intolerable.

Yet the subsequent institutional response concentrated on defending the reasonableness of the longitudinal clinical pathway and ultimately concluded that there had been **“no deficit in the care provided.”** The material presently identified does not demonstrate a separate institutional effort to examine the preceding psychological danger, establish whether an opportunity to prevent it had been missed, or offer a patient-safety response directed to the harm that had already occurred.

This brings the case together.

The first question is **how an integrated neurological and painful-foot presentation was separated into explanatory categories in 2017**.

The second is **whether an unresolved and contested clinical question was given documentary authority through the discharge synthesis and allowed to govern later interpretation**.

The third is **whether subsequent evidence of medication-responsive foot symptoms was adequately integrated into the clinical understanding and treatment of the condition**.

But the fourth, and ultimately most serious human question, is **how a patient who had lived with foot pain for thirteen years without comparable psychological distress came, during the subsequent treatment years, to express a death wish and an unwillingness to continue living with the suffering, without any separately identifiable patient-safety pathway being documented in response**.

That fourth question does not depend on proving the original neuropathy hypothesis wrong. It does not depend on proving every episode of pain was dystonia. And it does not disappear if some treatment decisions are judged reasonable.

**Even if every diagnostic disagreement were resolved in the hospital's favour, the adequacy of the response to documented, potentially life-threatening psychological distress would remain a separate question requiring an answer.**

The governing allegation is therefore not simply that a diagnosis was missed or a medication was delayed. It is that an unresolved clinical relationship became a durable management framework; the patient's suffering changed under neurological treatment; repeated evidence called for reassessment; and, when that suffering became an explicit threat to his ability to continue living, the available record does not demonstrate a corresponding patient-safety response.

**The case begins with the clinical handling of my feet, but it culminates in the handling of my safety.**

A comprehensive assessment should also examine separately held crisis, psychiatric, safeguarding and primary-care records. Treatment-state associations do not themselves prove that medication caused the later deterioration.*

## 2. What I mean by patienthood

My condition did not begin when I entered a neurology ward. My feet had a history before that admission. But my *patienthood*—the specific experience of having my condition interpreted and managed by a continuing neurological service—took a new form in July 2017.

Patienthood is not a diagnosis. It is the relationship between a person, a body in difficulty, a clinical explanation, a treatment pathway and the institution that keeps the record. Once the neurologist identified Parkinsonism, prescribed medication and produced the discharge account, subsequent observations of my feet occurred inside a particular clinical framework. What I reported, what was noticed, what was classified, what treatment was offered and what remained unresolved all became part of that continuing relationship.

This is why it is not sufficient to compare my foot pain in 2004 with my foot pain in 2025 as though the only variable were the natural course of disease. Between those points I was investigated, classified, medicated, reassessed, reassured, referred and answered. Some of that care was evidently active and skilled. It nevertheless altered the conditions in which I experienced and understood the pain.

The records do not establish that neurological medication *created* the later psychological distress. They do establish clinically important treatment-state relationships, while my own account establishes a difference in the character of the suffering before and after the commencement of neurological treatment. Whether some of the later distress was avoidable is a further clinical and legal question, not an assumption built into the history.

A case concerned with patienthood asks a question different from whether the first diagnosis was correct: **what was the cumulative effect of being treated through this particular interpretation, especially when evidence arose that might have changed it?**

## 3. Personhood: what the clinical categories did not adequately describe

The phrase *foot pain* does not convey the whole condition. A clinical note can preserve pain intensity, location, burning, corns, calluses, medication dose and gait abnormality without fully recording what the experience has done to the person's life.

For approximately thirteen years I had learned to live with painful feet without the degree of psychological distress that subsequently developed. This is not a claim that the earlier pain was mild. It is a distinction between physical suffering that I could live alongside and suffering that came to threaten my capacity to continue living at all.

By 2023, the GP had recorded the words **“causing death wish.”** By March 2025, my account of mornings included approximately 150 steps experienced as torture, and language expressing that I was not willing to continue with life under those conditions. The significance of those entries is not reducible to their usefulness as evidence of pain severity. They disclose a change in my relationship to life itself.

This is the personhood dimension of the case. It concerns mobility, autonomy, endurance, trust, the ability to work or participate, the prospect of another morning, and the diminishing space between suffering and survival. A person can receive repeated prescriptions and appointments while those larger dimensions remain insufficiently recognised.

Nor should psychological distress be treated as evidence that the pain was psychogenic or that the underlying foot problem was imaginary. **The distress was a response to suffering; it was also a clinical concern in its own right.** It called for attention regardless of whether the precise anatomical source of every painful sensation could be established.

## 4. Disability and aggravation: the case need not begin with causation of the original illness

I am not alleging that the hospital caused Parkinsonism or caused all of the painful foot problems that predated the admission. The distinction between original condition and potential *aggravation* is essential.

Disability here includes more than the movement disorder considered in isolation. It includes painful or abnormal feet, toe posture, gait disturbance, medication-related fluctuations, loss of ordinary function, and the psychological consequences of severe persistent suffering. Those features may have several mechanisms. Their practical effects, however, converged in one person.

The potential aggravation case has at least three dimensions.

First, was a pre-existing painful-foot condition made harder to manage because the relationship between pain, gait, tone and abnormal movement was inadequately investigated or framed? Second, when the neurological medication state demonstrably changed aspects of the foot experience, were transitions and deterioration adequately anticipated, monitored and addressed? Third, once distress became severe enough to generate explicit expressions of a death wish, was the patient exposed to avoidable additional harm through an insufficient safeguarding response?

These are not interchangeable allegations. The first concerns the original clinical formulation. The second concerns longitudinal treatment and reassessment. The third concerns the response to danger at the point it was recognised. Each requires its own evidential threshold.

The 2017–2018 procyclidine withdrawal episode is important in this analysis. When the medication was stopped, the later record describes the reported return of an outside-left-foot corn phenomenon alongside worsening tension, restlessness and movement. This is a single reported episode, not two independent trials, and it does not prove that the medication altered the tissue of a corn. But it put a foot phenomenon and the neurological treatment state into the same clinical observation. That was a reason to investigate the relationship, rather than to assume the original separation had been confirmed. (CLIN-001770–001790; CLIN-001914–001918.)

The later observations have comparable significance. In 2020 a footdrop-like movement was assessed as actually Parkinsonian after strength testing; in 2023 movement and pain were considered possibly OFF-related; by March 2025 foot pain was recorded as easing when Parkinson's medication took effect. None establishes that every foot symptom had the same cause. Together they create a longitudinal question about whether opportunities to relieve suffering were recognised and acted upon in time. (CLIN-002284–002288; CLIN-002574–002583; CLIN-002768–002800.)

An avoidable-harm case must ultimately specify *which additional suffering, over what period, would probably have been prevented by which reasonable intervention*. The fact that the initial illness was not caused by the hospital does not answer that question.

## 5. Construction: how the clinical account came to govern the case

The foundational documentary question begins before the discharge. The GP had referred a combined presentation: painful and abnormal feet, toe clawing, strikingly abnormal gait, increased tone, difficulty initiating movement and deterioration in function. This was an open relational problem. Neurology identified Parkinsonism and examined it seriously. But identification of one important diagnosis did not, by itself, determine the relationship of that diagnosis to the painful feet.

The surviving July 2017 documents show how a distinction I described was reformulated. In the neurologist's handwriting, I had **“began to be aware”** that foot pain and leg tension **“weren't the same.”** In the typed history I had **“began to realise”** they were **“not the same problem.”** In the final impression I was described as having **“markedly divorced”** the two **“in [my] own mind.”** The final account also introduced the possibility of completely separate foot symptoms or secondary peripheral neuropathy, potentially alcohol-related, while explicitly saying **“Not clear.”** (CLIN-000965–000976; CLIN-001142–001149.)

There is a difference between recognising two experiences and identifying two etiologies. The handwritten notes are not a verbatim recording of everything said, so an additional conversation or further reasoning may explain the stronger wording. That possibility must remain open. The documentary question is whether that additional basis can be identified and whether an interpretation was attributed to me without preserving the distinction between my report and the clinician's hypothesis.

There was another potentially decisive event. I recall objecting before discharge that the feet—the problem through which the wider clinical presentation had first been brought to attention—had not been adequately addressed. I recall asking for the neurologist and using an analogy about a vehicle passed through an inspection despite bald tyres. Four medical students, I say, were present. This is my evidence of the exchange; their identities, recollections and any contemporaneous traces have not been independently established in the material reviewed.

If that account is corroborated, it changes the clinical context of the final synthesis. The objection would have given explicit notice of a perceived failure to resolve the original problem before the account was completed. The issue would no longer be only an unnoticed omission but what was done after the patient brought the unresolved matter back to the clinician's attention.

The discharge account had particular authority because it assembled the admission into a patient-facing specialist explanation. It was intended to consolidate discussion and communicate a plan, and the assembled chronology indicates that it incorporated some post-discharge information. Its **“Plan/Recommendations: As per above”** formulation referred forward to the preceding account, including an uncertain disposition of the feet. That did not transform a possible neuropathy into a confirmed diagnosis. It potentially allowed an unresolved allocation to act as the starting framework for subsequent care.

The reported protest is not identified in the surviving specialist synthesis. That absence may be an omission requiring explanation; it is not, without further evidence, proof of deliberate suppression. What matters is that the finished account preserved the speculative explanation but not the patient's reported contemporaneous challenge to the completeness of the investigation.

## 6. Reconstruction: evidence arrived, but did the model change?

Medical explanations should be capable of revision when subsequent evidence bears upon their foundations. The central longitudinal concern is not that clinicians failed to notice every subsequent detail. Rather, the record shows many local responses while leaving uncertain whether the original relationship was ever reconstructed as a whole.

The treating neurologist remained involved. This was not simply a case in which a later clinician inherited an unfamiliar predecessor's letter. The person best placed to know that the neuropathy proposition began as **“perhaps”** and **“Not clear”** also had access, through the continuing treatment relationship, to observations that could test it. The availability of a particular individual note to him at a particular date must still be established rather than assumed.

A procyclidine withdrawal report connected changes in the left foot with changes in neurological tension and gait. A GP later described the feet in relation to increased tone and an abnormal Parkinsonian gait. Neurology itself later classified a footdrop-like phenomenon as Parkinsonian. Movement-disorders records discussed OFF-related pain and shuffling. The March 2025 account described severe morning foot pain improving as Parkinson's medication took effect and led to controlled-release Sinemet. (CLIN-001914–001918; CLIN-003091–003107; CLIN-002284–002288; CLIN-002574–002583; CLIN-002768–002800.)

These facts did not make peripheral neuropathy impossible. They did mean that the original separation required continued testing. A treatment may improve pain without proving that the anatomical origin of that pain is central; equally, a burning description or pregabalin response does not prove a peripheral nerve lesion. Both limits are important. The question is whether the competing explanations were ever explicitly brought back into relation, rather than left as parallel explanations which could each accommodate any new result.

There is a critical distinction between *local adaptation* and *global reconstruction*. Local adaptation changes a drug, responds to an OFF episode or treats a corn. Global reconstruction returns to the original problem and asks what the combined sequence now shows about the mechanism and management of the feet. The record reviewed demonstrates instances of the first; an equivalent source-by-source reconstruction of the second has not been identified.

That concern became explicit in the complaint and the subsequent response. The March 2025 neurologist's defence referred to **“neuropathic pain (of whatever cause)”**, even though his clinical documentation elsewhere continued to allow **“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here.”** These formulations do not occupy the same level of certainty. One treats the neuropathic category as available while keeping cause open; the other retains uncertainty about peripheral neuropathy itself. The significance is not a forbidden use of neuropathic terminology, but the need to account for what justified its status. (CLIN-002768–002800; CLIN-003239–003269.)

## 7. Concealment, omission and the ownership of the record

I use the word *concealment* cautiously. An omission can conceal a fact from a later reader without anyone deliberately intending to conceal it. A misleading record may result from compression, clinical assumptions, routine summarisation, an incomplete transcription, a failure to correct an earlier impression, or deliberate action. The documents do not presently establish which explanation accounts for each disputed passage.

Nevertheless, the **effect** of a record's omissions matters. If the report carries forward the proposition that I had mentally separated the two problems, while failing to preserve my reported protest that the feet remained unresolved, a later reader is presented with a different picture of what happened at the point of discharge.

The available record would suggest: the neurologist identified Parkinsonism; the feet may have been separate; the patient had markedly separated the experiences in his own mind; and the specialist plan proceeded accordingly. The competing account would add: the patient expressly objected that the investigation had not dealt with the feet, and the clinician continued toward discharge notwithstanding that challenge.

Those are different documentary histories. The second does not prove the treatment was negligent. But excluding it, if it occurred and was material, could affect what later clinicians, investigators and the patient himself were able to understand about the founding decision.

The later data-protection correspondence turns this into a concrete dispute about control of an authoritative record. Requests for access to provenance, rectification or completion, and restriction of reliance on contested information address different questions from a negligence claim. They ask whether a disputed personal-data record accurately reflects the relevant events, whether the competing account can travel with it, and how the institution uses it while its accuracy remains in dispute. These are questions about documentary accountability, not substitutes for proof of a clinical breach.

The 2025 institutional review is also relevant. A Serious Incident Management Team reportedly concluded that there had been **“no deficit in the care provided.”** The subsequent governance position stood over the outcome. Yet the reviewed material does not demonstrate whether the review independently reconstructed the full original GP referral, the alleged pre-discharge protest, the construction of the discharge synthesis and the treatment-state evidence, or whether it principally assessed decisions as they appeared within the record already being challenged.

This is a serious question about the *independence of the evidential starting point*. It should not be turned into an allegation that the reviewers knowingly concealed evidence without proof. The appropriate inquiry is to obtain and examine the Preliminary Assessment Report, its underlying evidence pack, review methodology, source selection, decisions, provenance and any account of the disputed documents.

The record should not become proof of its own completeness merely because subsequent decisions were made by reference to it.

## 8. Safeguarding: the point at which a diagnostic dispute became a question of immediate safety

The previous sections concern clinical interpretation, record formation and revision. Safeguarding is not merely the last step in that chain. It is a distinct duty of attention arising when the patient's suffering presents a risk to life or psychological safety.

The language recorded in 2023 matters. **“Causing death wish”** is not simply a more dramatic way of rating foot pain. It is a safety-relevant statement. The GP recognised seriousness, expressed worry and urgently sought neurological review **“before we get into trouble here.”** Those actions matter and should not be erased. But an urgent neurological referral is not necessarily equivalent to a documented assessment of suicide risk, a safety plan, crisis support or monitored psychological follow-up. (CLIN-002633–002641.)

The same distinction matters in March 2025, when the description of the first morning steps and the language of not being willing to live with the suffering showed how badly the experience had developed. Clinical adjustment to Sinemet CR was important, especially given the reported relief. But improvement in pain after a medication change does not itself retrospectively establish that the preceding period was safe, that expressed risk was assessed, or that the psychological consequences had been addressed. (CLIN-002768–002800.)

I am not claiming that no clinician ever acted with concern. The record contains meaningful attempts to address my symptoms. I am saying that **the material examined does not identify a coherent, separate patient-safety response proportionate to the explicit psychological warning signs**, and that the hospital's later defence has not visibly answered that question.

That is a claim which can be tested. An independent review should establish what risk assessments were made, what questions were asked, whether separate services were contacted, whether crisis advice or a safety plan was offered, what follow-up occurred, whether the GP's urgency was conveyed accurately and what the hospital's governance review actually considered. If an intervention occurred outside the reviewed materials, it should be identified and assessed. If none occurred, an expert should address what a reasonable response would have been at each relevant moment.

The concern is not solely retrospective. It is that in a continuous treatment relationship, **a warning about the patient's ability to remain alive with the suffering must be treated as information of a different order from an ordinary symptom update**. Even a medically defensible working diagnosis cannot displace that responsibility.

## 9. How the grounds connect—and where they remain distinct

This is not a bundle of unrelated grievances. Each issue describes one stage or consequence of a single lived trajectory:

1. **Original clinical problem.** An integrated neurological and painful-foot presentation entered the hospital in 2017, with the relationship between movement, gait, tone, toe posture and pain still open.
2. **Clinical and documentary construction.** The hospital identified Parkinsonism, formulated an uncertain separate explanation for painful feet, strengthened the recorded attribution of separation to me, and incorporated the account into the discharge plan.
3. **A contemporaneous challenge.** I recall explicitly pointing out before discharge that the feet had not been dealt with. The event requires corroboration, and its omission from the final specialist account requires explanation.
4. **Continuous treatment and potential aggravation.** Kemadrin and later dopaminergic drugs changed the foot experience. The subsequent record generated evidence capable of testing the original separation, raising questions about whether treatment and clinical reconstruction kept pace with what was being learned.
5. **Change in suffering and disability.** Longstanding foot pain, previously experienced without comparable psychological distress, became a state of intolerable suffering under continuing care. This development cannot simply be equated with the original disease's natural history without investigation.
6. **Explicit danger and safeguarding.** In 2023 and 2025 the record contained statements bearing directly on death, survival and inability to endure. The adequacy of the response is a question of its own, whether or not the original diagnostic formulation was defensible.
7. **Institutional reconstruction and accountability.** When I challenged the longitudinal care, the hospital defended it. What remains to be established is whether its review independently examined the founding record, the changing suffering, the treatment-state evidence and the safeguarding question, rather than merely validating treatment decisions as already documented.

These grounds have different evidential and legal consequences. Documentary discrepancy is not by itself clinical negligence. An omitted protest is not automatically misconduct. Medication responsiveness is not proof of a single pathology. Psychological deterioration under care does not by itself prove that the care caused it. And the absence of a safety entry from records reviewed is not yet proof that no safety intervention occurred.

But these cautions do not dissolve the case. They define what an independent examination has to determine.

A clinical-negligence analysis must ask what care was reasonably required, when, and what avoidable harm resulted from any shortfall. A professional-recordkeeping analysis asks whether clinical observations, patient statements, uncertainty and material disagreement were represented faithfully. A data-protection analysis asks how contested information was held, corrected, qualified and relied upon. A governance analysis asks whether the institution reviewed the disputed reasoning independently and responded adequately to the reported safety danger. The inquiries may inform one another without becoming interchangeable.

The overarching question is therefore not whether the hospital caused my original condition. It is **what happened to my existing condition, disability and psychological safety while I was under its care, how its clinical and documentary decisions influenced that trajectory, and what it did when the danger became explicit**.

I do not ask for the original record to be rewritten as though clinicians in 2017 possessed knowledge acquired in 2025 or 2026. I ask for the founding decisions to be examined using the information reasonably available at the time; for later decisions to be examined using what had become known by then; for gaps between those states of knowledge to be identified; and for the institutional account to distinguish what was investigated from what was merely hypothesised.

**My illness preceded the hospital. My patienthood developed within its care. My suffering became psychologically intolerable during that continuing relationship. The grounds of my case concern whether the care and the institution adequately accounted for that development and responded to it when my safety was at stake.**

## 11. Pain, suffering and danger are three different evidential states

**Pain** names the physical experience. I knew pain before the hospital episode. The fact that I could live with it without the later form of psychological distress is important: my original pain cannot simply be used to explain away everything that happened afterwards.

**Suffering** concerns the lived consequences: walking, work, everyday participation, diminished mobility, uncertain medication states, exhaustion, diminished autonomy and the gradual shrinking of a life. This is where *personhood* and *disability* enter the case. Disability is not exhausted by a diagnostic code or an examination finding; its effects are experienced through the body, environment, activity and relationships. The essay should neither romanticise what I endured before 2017 nor confuse endurance with absence of harm.

**Psychological danger** is a further threshold. The 2023 GP's “causing death wish” and concern “before we get into trouble here” are not interchangeable with a routine pain score. Nor do they, without assessment, establish the precise degree of suicidal intention or the intervention a clinician was necessarily required to provide. They **do** show that a healthcare professional communicated a serious warning requiring accountable clinical consideration. The question is what the receiving service understood, assessed, did and documented.

These distinctions also protect causation analysis. An expert might conclude that some of the change arose from the progression of the underlying disorder, some from treatment effects or insufficient symptom control, some from structural foot disease, and some from circumstances not yet examined. The claim is not that one cause must explain everything. It is that a potentially avoidable **increment of suffering**, and any failure to respond to safety-relevant deterioration, must be investigated rather than absorbed into the baseline disability.

## 12. Patienthood: what the service knew, and when it knew it

Patienthood is more than being assigned a diagnosis. It is the history of becoming legible to healthcare through examinations, accounts, treatment responses, summaries and decisions. My own observations remained a continuous information source. The record's changing account of me and my body's changing response to treatment should therefore be read together.

This was a small and continuous clinical network. The same GP remained involved from 2017; the same neurologist remained involved with the same patient and the specialist account he had authored or approved. Later movement-disorders nursing input entered that network. As [*Lecture 4 — The Small Network*](https://github.com/TitanicParker/case-readable/blob/main/small-network.md) puts it: **the neurologist did not inherit his own record**.

That is important for two opposing reasons. It means recurring labels cannot automatically be treated as independent confirmation from strangers. It also means the strongest response for the hospital may be that precisely this continuity permitted informed treatment adjustments and reasonable specialist judgment. The issue cannot be settled by the existence of continuity alone.

Instead, at each material stage, the inquiry needs a knowledge ledger: what facts were available; which clinician received them; what interpretation they placed on them; what investigation or treatment followed; what danger was communicated; and whether new information materially altered the governing model. An entry in a GP note cannot automatically be attributed as personally read by a particular neurologist. But a neurologist's own 2018 or 2025 account can be examined as evidence of what that neurologist recorded and understood.

There is a particular methodological gap worth investigating. Neurology had recognised dystonic-looking movement in 2017, and later PRKN evidence joined dystonia with Parkinsonism. The question is not whether dystonia was *ever mentioned*. It is whether its **extent and prominence in the lower-limb phenotype** were examined sufficiently for the treatment decisions being made. [*Lecture 5 — The Missing Phenotype*](https://github.com/TitanicParker/case-readable/blob/main/operational-model.md) asks this without projecting the 2026 genotype back into 2017. **Presence is not predominance.**

## 13. The patient-safety ground must survive the strongest hospital answer

There was real care. In 2023 the GP made an urgent referral, the movement-disorders service engaged, medications were considered and adjusted, and neurological explanations including OFF phenomena were discussed. It would be inaccurate to say that nobody responded to the pain or that nothing was done. These interventions may themselves have been relevant to reducing psychological risk.

The specific safety allegation is narrower and more testable:

> **Despite documented statements indicating serious psychological distress and potential danger, the records reviewed have not identified a distinct, adequately documented assessment and response to the patient's psychological safety. The question is whether the care actually provided was proportionate to the risk reasonably presented and adequately communicated between the responsible services.**

The hospital's strongest reasonable response is that not every expression of intolerable suffering demonstrates imminent suicidal intent, that medical treatment aimed at relieving the pain was itself a protective response, and that the appropriate clinical assessment may have occurred within ordinary consultations or in records not yet examined. That response must be taken seriously. It is not a complete answer simply because treatment occurred; nor is the absence of a document a complete demonstration that the required assessment did not occur.

The evidence needed includes the full GP and hospital notes, referral triage records, telephone and nursing records, any structured or unstructured risk assessment, safety-net advice, communications to the GP, crisis or liaison-psychiatry contact if any, appointment and follow-up chronology, and evidence of what was told directly to each service. An independent clinician with appropriate expertise should assess the response against the patient's presentation at the time, not against a retrospective assumption that any particular pathway was compulsory.

There are at least two questions here, and they should remain separate. **First**, did the distress create another reason to re-evaluate the neurological and foot-pain model? **Second**, irrespective of that model's correctness, was the patient adequately protected when the distress itself became safety-relevant? The second question survives an expert finding that the diagnostic and pharmacological decisions were reasonable.

This deliberately adopts the adversarial standard in [*Adversarial stress-test*, Proposition 10](https://github.com/TitanicParker/case-readable/blob/main/adversarial.md): the broad allegation of *no clinical response* is contradicted by the record; the specific adequacy-of-protection question remains open to evidence and expert assessment.

## 14. Aggravation and causation: replace the single long leap with identifiable counterfactuals

No satisfactory analysis can infer that the 2017 discharge wording alone *caused* the 2023 crisis. The interval contains a progressive neurological disorder, structural foot problems, many treatment decisions, mixed treatment responses and evolving life circumstances. A rigorous legal case must identify a particular departure from reasonable care, a credible alternative course, and an incremental outcome that the alternative would probably have prevented or mitigated.

The main candidate decision points are these:

| Decision point | The question for an expert | Counterfactual to test | Evidential limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| July 2017 | Was the integrated feet–gait–tone presentation investigated, framed and handed over adequately, especially given the reported protest? | A targeted integrated examination, an express unresolved question, a documented plan for review or a reasoned decision not to investigate further. | The original peripheral explanation was uncertain, not demonstrably false. The protest is patient testimony pending further corroboration. |
| 2017–2018 treatment-state evidence | Did the recorded procyclidine withdrawal association justify focused enquiry, monitoring or a different treatment plan? | Comparing symptoms and medication states over time, assessing toe/gait mechanics and foot pain together, and monitoring changes when treatment was altered. | One episode cannot prove that procyclidine prevented callus formation or that withdrawing it caused a long-term injury. |
| 2019–2023 accumulating evidence | Were findings linking pain, gait and Parkinsonian tone/OFF phenomena integrated across services? | Formal re-characterisation of foot symptoms and an explicitly revised multidisciplinary plan. | The record contains genuine local reconsideration and treatments, not simple inactivity. |
| 2023 warning | What clinical psychological-risk assessment and protective response were reasonably required by the GP's warning? | Timely risk enquiry, safety-netting, follow-up, coordinated treatment or referral as indicated by the findings. | The text is serious but does not alone establish imminent intent, mandatory psychiatric admission or negligent omission. |
| March 2025 and afterwards | What did the reported Sinemet CR relief establish about earlier therapeutic assumptions, and what review of residual harm or risk was indicated? | A documented retrospective review of treatment-state pain, current safety, ongoing management and unresolved explanatory questions. | Later benefit does not prove the same regimen was safely indicated at the same time in earlier years. |
| 2025–2026 institutional review | Did the complaint and SIMT process examine the grounds raised, including the foundational record and safety response? | Independent source-to-synthesis review, clear treatment of contested facts and a reasoned response to each issue. | An inadequate review, if shown, does not itself establish that earlier clinical decisions caused injury. |

The legal analysis must distinguish *causing the original condition*, *aggravating the condition*, *failing to relieve avoidable suffering*, *failing to protect against identifiable psychological risk*, and *subsequently mishandling the complaint or personal data*. These are not interchangeable routes to compensation. Appropriate Irish legal advice and expert evidence are needed to identify which, if any, constitute viable causes of action, the responsible parties, the limitation issues, and the recoverable losses.

## 15. Construction, reconstruction, omission and concealment: the evidential ladder

The disputed record has at least two roles. It is an account of the illness and care; it is also **one of the objects whose making and later use must be examined**. The integrated GP question was compressed. The neurologist's handwritten account was reformulated more strongly in the typed record. The final impression entertained a peripheral explanation and attributed an even stronger separation to the patient. “As per above” referred the formal plan back to that synthesis. The later clinical course was documented within a continuing network.

The record may have preserved individual facts while weakening their relationships. In the words of [*Lecture 5*](https://github.com/TitanicParker/case-readable/blob/main/operational-model.md), **facts can survive compression while relationships do not**. This is a better description of the allegation than “the feet were never seen”: the examination did record foot structure and abnormal gait. The question is whether the *relationship* the GP had referred was adequately investigated and retained.

The dispute should be stated using a ladder of increasing evidential demands:

1. **Omission:** the completed discharge synthesis does not record my reported protest. That omission is observable; proof of what happened at the bedside depends on testimony and other sources.
2. **Material incompleteness or misleading effect:** did the omission, combined with stronger attributed wording, alter how subsequent readers understood what had been investigated, challenged and left unresolved? This is an inference requiring examination of provenance, purpose and later use.
3. **Institutional continued reliance:** what did the hospital actually have before it during the 2025 PAR/SIMT process, and what independent reconstruction did it perform before reaching “no deficit”? The outcome exists; the full source pack and methodology remain critical to assessment.
4. **Deliberate concealment:** did any identifiable person knowingly suppress an important contrary fact, and for what purpose? Neither the wording differences nor an omitted objection, without more, establish that state of mind.

The 2017 discharge narrative may be clinically sophisticated and still require this audit. A good-faith explanation can become an operational model whose later use exceeds the strength of the evidence first supporting it. Equally, proof that the clinicians considered competing mechanisms and reasonably integrated later evidence could substantially defeat the claim of closed-minded persistence.

The hospital's strongest counter-case should therefore be recorded in the essay rather than confined to a rebuttal: genuine structural lesions existed; painful phenomena could have several causes; dystonic-looking movement was recognised; the treating team sought specialist input, investigated and treated actively; levodopa carried patient-specific risks; later clinicians did undertake some genuine reassessment; and the eventual genotype could not have been known in 2017. These points weaken caricatures of negligence, but they do not automatically establish the source-to-synthesis bridge or the adequacy of the safety response.

## 16. The legal cases arising from the grounds

What follows is a **map of issues for solicitors and appropriate experts**, not a legal opinion or a declaration that each route is a free-standing compensable claim.

**Clinical negligence.** Identify specific duties, allegedly deficient decisions, the professional standard at each date, foreseeable consequences, what reasonable treatment or assessment would have entailed, and the probability of incremental harm being avoided. The clinical pathway contains possible investigatory, medication-management, longitudinal-integration and safeguarding questions. These should not be pleaded as one undifferentiated eight-year failure.

**Aggravation and psychological injury.** Establish the pre-2017 baseline, subsequent pain and functional trajectory, any clinically identifiable psychiatric or psychological injury, and the distinction between disease progression and avoidable exacerbation. The GP's documented warning is powerful evidence of distress, but legal causation and the extent of recoverable psychological injury require expert analysis.

**Safeguarding and safe clinical response.** Ask whether the 2023 and 2025 communications triggered reasonable risk assessment, follow-up or protective action, and identify precisely which service knew what. This may be part of a negligence claim or a separate professional/governance concern, depending on evidence and legal advice.

**Professional records and patient communication.** Examine the accuracy of the attributed patient understanding, the retention of uncertainty, the documentation of the reported protest, and whether the patient was informed not merely *within* the framework but *about* its uncertainty and contestability. These questions can matter independently of monetary compensation.

**Data protection and documentary integrity.** The stated accuracy, completion, access/provenance, restriction and continued-reliance disputes require their own analysis under the applicable data-protection framework. A medical opinion is not automatically an objectively inaccurate factual datum, and a request to attach a supplementary patient account is distinct from rewriting a historical note. The regulatory process does not decide medical negligence.

**Institutional complaint and governance review.** Test whether the March 2025 complaint's broad structural questions were answered by the treating neurologist, by PAR/SIMT and by later QPS communications, or whether the inquiry shifted toward defending specific treatment decisions. Compare the question asked with the question answered at every stage. Institutional validation of care is evidence of an institutional conclusion, not independent proof that the underlying premises were correct.

**Personhood and disability.** These are indispensable descriptions of what happened to the individual and may inform injury, disability, dignity, participation and remedial analysis. They are not, on their own, substitutes for identifying a recognised legal cause of action and its constituent elements.

## 17. The adversarial test: what would actually weaken or defeat the case?

A persuasive case should state in advance what a successful answer by the hospital would look like. If the hospital can establish a clinically sound, documented division between the foot-pain mechanisms and the movement disorder, the foundation of the diagnostic-partition allegation weakens. If it can show a focused response to the reported protest, the closure-under-notice allegation weakens. If contemporaneous examinations and reasoning demonstrate repeated, genuinely independent reconstruction of the lower-limb model, the contention of uncorrected anchoring may be substantially defeated.

If the hospital can produce evidence that the 2023 and 2025 psychological warnings were understood, assessed for relevant risk, appropriately safety-netted and followed up, then the specific safeguarding allegation may be defeated—even if a separate form entitled “safeguarding” was never completed. And if independent expert review supports the reasonableness of the treatment decisions and concludes that earlier alternative care would not probably have altered the course of suffering, a compensation claim premised on avoidable aggravation becomes substantially harder.

Conversely, if the response is only that Parkinsonism was diagnosed, possible neuropathy was considered, treatment occurred and the later review endorsed the treatment, important questions remain unanswered. Those facts do not by themselves demonstrate how the original explanatory separation was earned, how later evidence tested it, or how escalating psychological danger was considered.

The essay's governing test is therefore not whether the hospital can tell a plausible clinical story. It is whether it can produce the **evidential bridges** between what was observed, what was inferred, what was communicated, what was done, and what was later claimed to have been resolved.

## 18. The convergence: the history of the person under care

My illness preceded the hospital. My pain also preceded it. What followed was not simply more of the same. I became a patient within an enduring explanatory and therapeutic relationship in which my feet, movement, medication responses, disability and psychological endurance were repeatedly made available for clinical interpretation.

The relevant question is not whether I can now name every mechanism that caused every painful experience. It is whether the service responded appropriately as my experience changed and as its own evidence accumulated. When my capacity to live with the suffering became a documented concern, the obligation to recognise me as a person at risk could not be exhausted by describing the pain as neuropathic, Parkinsonian, structural or mixed.

The documentary branch, clinical branch, aggravation branch and safety branch all converge here, but none should be allowed to prove the others by repetition. A deficient record does not automatically prove deficient care. Pain responsive to medication does not automatically prove an earlier breach. A death-wish statement does not automatically prove that clinicians ignored it. An institutional conclusion does not automatically prove that the patient's safety was adequately considered.

**This is a case about how a person's longstanding pain developed under medical care into psychologically intolerable suffering; what the system knew at each stage; what it could reasonably have done differently; and whether, at the point of documented danger, it protected the person whose medical history it had been so carefully recording.**
